### Section 11.2: Spectrum and Station Sites

Where you put a station can change how you may operate. A repeater may share spectrum with radar, a home may sit near a protected receiver, and an antenna may lie in an airport's approach area. Check those conditions before treating a band allocation as the whole answer.

#### Who Else Uses the Frequency?

Amateurs share the 70-centimeter band with radiolocation services. *If a repeater interferes with a protected radiolocation system, continuing at the same settings is not an option.*

> **Key Information:** A 70-centimeter repeater that interferes with a radiolocation system must cease operation or make changes that mitigate the interference. {{< link id="E1B04" >}}

A fix must address the actual interference. You may need to change frequency, power, antenna pattern, or filtering. The methods from Chapter 9 help find the cause. You must still protect the other service while doing that work.

Location also matters near Canada. *Line A* is a defined boundary inside the United States, not the international border itself.

> **Key Information:**
> - Line A runs roughly parallel to and south of the US–Canada border. {{< link id="E1F04" >}}
> - Amateur stations in the contiguous 48 states north of Line A may not transmit from 420 to 430 MHz. {{< link id="E1F05" >}}

Use the actual boundary in §97.3 when checking a site. “Near Canada” helps you remember the purpose, but it is not a map for deciding whether your antenna site lies north of the line.

#### What Receivers Need Protection?

FCC monitoring facilities must hear signals well enough to do their work. A nearby amateur transmitter can overload a monitoring receiver even when the amateur's transmission is otherwise clean.

> **Key Information:** An amateur station within 1 mile of an FCC monitoring facility must protect it from harmful interference. {{< link id="E1B03" >}}

Radio astronomy has another need: receiving extremely weak natural signals. The National Radio Quiet Zone spans parts of Maryland, Virginia, and West Virginia. *It surrounds Green Bank and other protected receiving sites.*

> **Key Information:** The National Radio Quiet Zone is an area surrounding the National Radio Astronomy Observatory. {{< link id="E1B05" >}}

That is the pool's name for the protected astronomy site at Green Bank, now operated as Green Bank Observatory. Check the rules for your site. The zone's name does not mean that all radio use throughout it is forbidden.

A broadcast receiver can also qualify for protection. Section 97.121 covers certain interference to receivers of good engineering design. *It limits use of the frequencies causing that interference during specified hours.*

> **Key Information:** For interference to domestic broadcast reception using receivers of good engineering design, the FCC may require an amateur station to avoid transmitting during certain hours on the offending frequencies. {{< link id="E1B08" >}}

This limits the troublesome operation without banning every band. Find the path the interference follows and correct its source. A clean transmitter alone may not solve the problem.

#### Which Authority Governs the Structure?

A tower's effect on aircraft and its effect on local land use are separate questions. Approval under one set of rules does not settle the other.

> **Key Information:**
> - An amateur antenna structure at or near a public-use airport may require FAA notification and FCC registration under Part 17. {{< link id="E1B06" >}}
> - PRB-1 applies to state and local zoning regulations. {{< link id="E1B07" >}}
> - PRB-1 requires reasonable accommodation of amateur radio in state and local rules affecting antenna size and structures. {{< link id="E1B11" >}}

Reasonable accommodation does not mean you may put a tower of any height anywhere. PRB-1 also calls for local rules to impose only the minimum practical limits needed to serve a legitimate local purpose. It does not itself override private homeowners-association covenants or aircraft safety rules. Keep those approvals separate. A county zoning approval, for example, does not settle a private covenant or an aircraft-safety requirement.
