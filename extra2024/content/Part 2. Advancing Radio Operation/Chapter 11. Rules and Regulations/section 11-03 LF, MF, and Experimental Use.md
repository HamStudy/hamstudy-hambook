### Section 11.3: LF, MF, and Experimental Use

On 2200 and 630 meters, permission starts with the station's fixed location. These bands overlap frequencies used by power-line carrier (PLC) systems, which send control information along utility transmission lines. The notification process helps keep amateur transmissions away from nearby systems using the same frequencies.

#### Notify Before Transmitting

The 2200-meter band covers 135.7–137.8 kHz; 630 meters covers 472–479 kHz. Before using either, identify the band or bands you intend to use and the coordinates of the transmitting antenna's fixed location.

> **Key Information:**
> - Before transmitting on 2200 or 630 meters, notify the Utilities Technology Council, or UTC, of your call sign and station coordinates. {{< link id="E1C07" >}}
> - You may begin after 30 days if UTC has not told you that the station is within 1 kilometer of a PLC system using the same or overlapping frequencies. {{< link id="E1C03" >}}

The notification also includes the intended band or bands. Mark the *30-day waiting period* in your plans. You do not need to wait indefinitely for an approval letter, but you do need to meet the stated separation condition. Operation remains prohibited within the specified separation distance from a transmission line carrying an overlapping PLC signal.

The rule still uses the older name “Utilities Telecom Council.” The pool uses the organization's current name, Utilities Technology Council. Both refer to UTC in this process.

#### Limit Radiated Power

Short antennas at these long wavelengths can have very low efficiency. That makes transmitter output and radiated power quite different numbers. Here, the letters after “watts” matter as much as the number. The EIRP limits account for the power reaching the antenna and its gain relative to an isotropic radiator.

> **Key Information:**
> - The maximum radiated power on 2200 meters is 1 W EIRP. {{< link id="E1A07" >}}
> - On 630 meters, the maximum is 5 W EIRP except in the specified parts of Alaska. {{< link id="E1A09" >}}

In Alaska, stations within 800 kilometers of the Russian Federation are limited to 1 W EIRP on 630 meters. Separate transmitter limits also apply: 1500 W PEP on 2200 meters and 500 W PEP on 630 meters. Meeting the transmitter limit alone does not establish compliance with the much lower EIRP limit.

For example, if 100 W reaches an antenna with a gain of −20 dBi, its power gain as a ratio is 0.01. The EIRP is $100\times0.01=1$ W. Use a defensible antenna-gain estimate rather than assuming that an inefficient-looking antenna must be below the limit.

The 630-meter allocation is narrow, but it is not reserved entirely for CW and data.

> **Key Information:** Phone emissions are permitted throughout the 630-meter band. {{< link id="E1C12" >}}

The complete emission still must fit within 472–479 kHz and meet its applicable standards. A phone signal near either edge needs the same bandwidth planning used earlier in this chapter.

#### A Separate Permission for Experiments

Most amateur experiments fit within normal license privileges. An experiment that requires operation beyond those privileges may need a separate authorization with its own frequencies, power, location, and time limits.

> **Key Information:** The FCC may issue Special Temporary Authority, or STA, to provide for experimental amateur communications. {{< link id="E1F06" >}}

An STA is permission for the specified activity, not a general expansion of an amateur license. Ordinary 2200- or 630-meter operation under the band rules and UTC notification process does not require one. Requesting an STA also does not authorize transmission before the FCC grants it.
