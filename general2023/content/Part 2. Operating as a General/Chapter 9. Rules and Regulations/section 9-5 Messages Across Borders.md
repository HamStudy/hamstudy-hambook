### Section 9.5: Messages Across Borders

An ordinary contact exchanges information between amateur operators. Relaying a message for someone else adds another consideration, and an international border may change which messages you can carry. Operating a transmitter located abroad is different again from contacting a foreign station from home.

#### Contacting an Amateur in Another Country

An international contact does not normally require a separate agreement between the two operators’ countries. The basic permission is broad, with a specific exception:

> **Key Information:** Amateur stations may communicate with amateurs in other countries except those whose administrations have notified the ITU that they object to such communications. {{< link id="G1B08" >}}

An international allocation also does not give every operator the same national privileges. The ITU divides the world into three radio regions:

> **Key Information:** ITU Region 2 frequency allocations apply to amateurs operating in North and South America. {{< link id="G1E06" >}}

The regional allocations give countries a shared framework. Within it, each country’s government decides which radio services may operate and what privileges their operators have. Consult the rules for the station’s location rather than assuming that every country in a region grants identical amateur access.

For example, a station you can hear may be permitted to transmit outside your own authorized segment. The split-frequency procedure from Section 7.3 can allow a contact when each station has a suitable transmit frequency.

#### Keeping the Message Understandable

CW and digital operators use abbreviations to send familiar information efficiently. The rules allow that shorthand without allowing messages intended to conceal their meaning:

> **Key Information:** Abbreviations and procedural signals may be used if they do not obscure a message’s meaning. {{< link id="G1B07" >}}

The Q-signals from the previous chapter are examples: QRS requests slower sending, and QSL acknowledges receipt and understanding. A listener may need to learn those conventions, but they are not a secret arrangement between the two operators.

Likewise, encoding data for transmission and error correction is different from encrypting a message to hide its contents. The purpose and applicable rules matter; a signal does not become prohibited merely because a particular listener lacks the software to decode it.

#### Sending Information for Someone Else

A *third-party communication* is a message passed between control operators on behalf of another person. It can involve reading someone’s written message, relaying an email, or allowing a guest to speak.

A guest may state a permitted message while the control operator remains at the control point, continuously monitoring and supervising. Some prior enforcement actions disqualify a person from participating this way:

> **Key Information:** A person whose amateur license has been revoked and not reinstated is disqualified from participating in sending a third-party message. {{< link id="G1E01" >}}

The rule also covers specified suspensions and other enforcement restrictions. The control operator remains responsible for the transmission even when someone else is speaking.

Within US jurisdiction, third-party messages are generally permitted subject to the normal content rules. Routine international third-party traffic requires an appropriate arrangement with the other country. An exception applies if the third party is eligible to be the station’s control operator. Check the current arrangements rather than assuming that permission for an ordinary international contact also permits a guest’s message.

Where an international third-party arrangement exists, the message still has limits:

> **Key Information:** Third-party messages sent to a country with a third-party agreement must concern amateur radio, remarks of a personal character, or emergencies or disaster relief. {{< link id="G1E05" >}}

A personal greeting is different from using the link for routine commercial business. A separate rule also allows international third-party communications for emergencies or disaster relief without an ordinary third-party arrangement. That exception does not give blanket permission for routine traffic during an exercise or an event labeled an emergency.

At the end of an international third-party exchange, the identification must include the other station’s callsign as well as your own.

#### Carrying Messages by Remote Control

Moving the control point away from the transmitter does not create a new category of permitted message:

> **Key Information:** Third-party messages may be transmitted by remote control whenever those messages are otherwise permitted by FCC rules. {{< link id="G1E12" >}}

For example, an operator controlling a station over an internet link can relay a permitted personal message. The content and destination restrictions still apply, as do any requirements for supervising a guest’s participation.

#### Identifying Which Country’s Station You Operate

For radio operating privileges, focus on where the transmitter is located. The operator and transmitter may be in different countries:

> **Key Information:**
> - To operate a US station by remote control from outside the country, the control operator must hold a US operator/primary station license. {{< link id="G1D05" >}}
> - When operating a station in South America by remote control from the US, the remote station’s country’s rules govern the amateur radio operation. {{< link id="G1D12" >}}

In the first case, the station remains a US station, operating under FCC rules and the control operator’s US privileges. Holding that US license does not mean every foreign location permits every form of remote access. Check any requirements that apply where you are staying too.

The reverse arrangement uses the station’s foreign authorization. A US General Class License does not automatically authorize you to use that station. Obtain whatever authorization the transmitter’s country requires and follow its frequency, power, identification, and control rules.

#### Taking a Radio Abroad

Taking the transmitter with you is not the same as remotely using a US station. A foreign country may offer reciprocal privileges, recognize a regional arrangement, or require an individual permit. Eligibility can depend on your license class as well as the destination, so a US General Class License is not automatically sufficient under every arrangement.

Before traveling, check the destination’s regulator or recognized national amateur organization for the current requirements. Confirm any documents, callsign prefix, power limits, and permitted bands.

From a home station, a remote control point, or a temporary location abroad, the same distinction remains useful: know which station you are authorized to control and what it may transmit. The final part of that responsibility is keeping the license itself valid and knowing when a new or upgraded privilege takes effect.
