### Section 9.5: Messages Across Borders

An ordinary contact exchanges information between amateur operators. Relaying a message for someone else adds another consideration, and an international border may change which messages you can carry. Operating a transmitter located abroad is different again from contacting a foreign station from home.

Keeping those situations separate helps you identify the rule you actually need: permission to contact the station, permission to carry the message, or authority to operate the transmitter.

#### Contacting an Amateur in Another Country

An international contact does not normally require a separate agreement between the two operators’ countries. The basic permission is broad, with a specific exception:

> **Key Information:** Amateur stations may communicate with amateurs in other countries except those whose administrations have notified the ITU that they object to such communications. {{< link id="G1B08" >}}

This is a rule about communication between amateur stations. It is not permission to take your radio into any country and transmit there, or to relay every kind of message across a border. The ordinary frequency, identification, and content rules still apply.

An international allocation also does not give every operator the same national privileges. The ITU divides the world into three radio regions:

> **Key Information:** ITU Region 2 frequency allocations apply to amateurs operating in North and South America. {{< link id="G1E06" >}}

The regional allocations provide a framework; national administrations authorize particular services and operator privileges within it. Consult the rules for the station’s location rather than assuming that every country in a region grants identical amateur access.

For example, a station you can hear may be permitted to transmit outside your own authorized segment. The split-frequency procedure from Section 7.3 can allow a contact when each station has a suitable transmit frequency. Neither operator gains the other’s privileges by making that contact.

#### Keeping the Message Understandable

CW and digital operators use abbreviations to send familiar information efficiently. The rules allow that shorthand without allowing messages intended to conceal their meaning:

> **Key Information:** Abbreviations and procedural signals may be used if they do not obscure a message’s meaning. {{< link id="G1B07" >}}

The Q-signals from the previous chapter are examples: QRS requests slower sending, and QSL acknowledges receipt and understanding. A listener may need to learn those conventions, but they are not a secret arrangement between the two operators.

Likewise, encoding data for transmission and error correction is different from encrypting a message to hide its contents. The purpose and applicable rules matter; a signal does not become prohibited merely because a particular listener lacks the software to decode it. Use publicly explained methods where required, and do not treat a digital link as a private channel exempt from amateur message rules.

#### Sending Information for Someone Else

A *third-party communication* is a message passed between control operators on behalf of another person. It can involve reading someone’s written message, relaying an email, or allowing a guest to speak. The use of voice or digital data does not by itself change its third-party status.

A guest may state a permitted message while the control operator remains at the control point, continuously monitoring and supervising. Some prior enforcement actions disqualify a person from participating this way:

> **Key Information:** A person whose amateur license has been revoked and not reinstated is disqualified from participating in sending a third-party message. {{< link id="G1E01" >}}

The rule also covers specified suspensions and other enforcement restrictions. A guest cannot use another operator’s station to avoid those restrictions. The control operator remains responsible for the transmission even when someone else is speaking.

Within US jurisdiction, third-party messages are generally permitted subject to the normal content rules. Routine international third-party traffic requires an appropriate arrangement with the other country, unless the exception for a third party eligible to be the station’s control operator applies. Check the current arrangements rather than assuming that permission for an ordinary international contact also permits a guest’s message.

Where an international third-party arrangement exists, the message still has limits:

> **Key Information:** Third-party messages sent to a country with a third-party agreement must concern amateur radio, remarks of a personal character, or emergencies or disaster relief. {{< link id="G1E05" >}}

A personal greeting is different from using the link for routine commercial business. There is also a separate allowance for international third-party emergency or disaster-relief communications without an ordinary third-party arrangement. That exception is not a general waiver for routine traffic during an exercise or an event labeled an emergency.

At the end of an international third-party exchange, the identification must include the other station’s callsign as well as your own. The practical message-handling skills from the previous chapter still apply: preserve the sender’s meaning, confirm what was received, and keep track of the message’s destination.


#### Carrying Messages by Remote Control

Moving the control point away from the transmitter does not create a new category of permitted message:

> **Key Information:** Third-party messages may be transmitted by remote control whenever those messages are otherwise permitted by FCC rules. {{< link id="G1E12" >}}

For example, an operator controlling a station over an internet link can relay a permitted personal message. The content and destination restrictions still apply, as do any requirements for supervising a guest’s participation. Remote control describes how the operator controls the station, not an exemption from responsibility.

#### Identifying Which Country’s Station You Operate

For radio operating privileges, focus on where the transmitter is located. The operator and transmitter may be in different countries:

> **Key Information:** To operate a US station by remote control from outside the country, the control operator must hold a US operator/primary station license. {{< link id="G1D05" >}}

The station remains a US station, operating under FCC rules and the control operator’s US privileges. This requirement does not establish that every foreign location permits every form of remote access; check any requirements that apply where you are staying too.

The reverse arrangement uses the station’s foreign authorization:

> **Key Information:** When operating a station in South America by remote control from the US, the remote station’s country’s rules govern the amateur radio operation. {{< link id="G1D12" >}}

A US General Class License does not automatically authorize you to use that station. Obtain whatever authorization the transmitter’s country requires and follow its frequency, power, identification, and control rules. The owner’s permission alone does not settle the licensing question.

#### Taking a Radio Abroad

Taking the transmitter with you is not the same as remotely using a US station. A foreign country may offer reciprocal privileges, recognize a regional arrangement, or require an individual permit. Eligibility can depend on your license class as well as the destination, so a US General Class License is not automatically sufficient under every arrangement.

Before traveling, check the destination’s regulator or recognized national amateur organization for the current requirements. Confirm any documents, callsign prefix, power limits, and permitted bands. Do not assume that being a guest at a local club station removes those requirements.

From a home station, a remote control point, or a temporary location abroad, the same distinction remains useful: know which station you are authorized to control and what it may transmit. The final part of that responsibility is keeping the license itself valid and knowing when a new or upgraded privilege takes effect.
